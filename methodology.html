How worldbook.earth is built

Every layer traces to named public sources. Nothing here is drawn from intuition.

Worldbook is a layered atlas of the world — 49 data layers you can switch on and off, spanning how the world connects (flows of money, goods, people, and information), how countries compare (development, politics, health, culture), and how populations have moved across four centuries of history. Every layer is drawn from named, public data. If something appears on the map, a source reports it. If a number sets a value, that number comes from the citation attached to the layer.

This page exists so you never have to take that on faith. Everything below is checkable.

The one rule: Every number traces to a named source. Geometry is not fabricated. Absence is not evidence of absence.

Three kinds of layer

Worldbook shows data three different ways, and they answer different questions:

Flow layers (animated arcs)

Great-circle arcs between countries: remittances, trade, oil & gas, grain, minerals, timber, aid, refugees, students, flights, shipping, submarine cables, and the illicit layers — drugs, arms, human trafficking, wildlife, counterfeit goods — plus natural systems (ocean currents, bird flyways, Saharan dust) and historic routes (Silk Road, Age of Exploration).

Choropleth layers (shaded countries)

Countries colored by a value: Human Development Index, GDP per capita, population density, internet use, CO₂ per capita, life expectancy, under-5 and maternal mortality, physicians per 1,000, health spending, HIV prevalence, water and sanitation access, government debt, plus categorical maps of religion, language family, climate, government type, regime type, and LGBTQ+ legal status.

Historical migration (animated over time)

A time-scrubbed layer of 221 individually-sourced migration edges spanning ~400 years — the trans-Atlantic slave trade, Partition of India, the Irish Famine emigration, European waves to the Americas, guest-worker programs, and modern refugee movements from Vietnam, Syria, Venezuela, and Myanmar. Each edge carries its own citation.

What the values mean — and what they don't

Not every layer measures the same kind of thing, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Broadly there are three tiers, and worldbook labels each in the app:

  1. Hard quantities. Remittances, debt, migration, trade, and the numeric choropleths (HDI, GDP, life expectancy, and the rest) carry real reported figures. An arc's thickness or a country's shade is proportional to that figure.
  2. Route prominence. Oil, grain, minerals, timber, flights, shipping, students, and refugees show the major documented corridors, weighted by relative scale — the principal flows, not an exhaustive census of every route.
  3. Relative, seizure-based signal. Human trafficking, wildlife trafficking, counterfeit goods, and medical brain drain encode relative corridor prominence — because the underlying data is detections, seizures, and reported cases, not a clean count of the true flow. An arc here means "this corridor shows up prominently in the data," not "this many units moved."

Submarine cables are shown at uniform weight; for fibre routes, thickness carries no meaning. The categorical choropleths (religion, regime type, LGBTQ+ status, etc.) show a classification, not a quantity — see below.

The most important caveat: absence is not evidence of absence

This applies hardest to the illicit layers. A thick arms-trafficking arc into a country means the Small Arms Survey and UNODC documented flows there. A thin arc, or no arc, very often means no data was reported — not that no trafficking occurs. Enforcement data reflects where monitoring, seizures, and reporting happen, which is uneven across the world for reasons unrelated to the true scale of the activity. Countries with strong reporting regimes can look "worse" precisely because they measure and disclose more.

So do not read these maps as moral rankings of countries. They are maps of where data exists — a real and useful thing to see, but not the same as a map of where the underlying activity happens, and worldbook does not claim it is.

Classifications, borders, and contested cases

Some layers assign each country to a category rather than a number — regime type, government type, religion, LGBTQ+ legal status, language family. These follow the classification of the cited source (V-Dem, EIU, Freedom House, ILGA World, Pew, and others). Worldbook reports what those sources report; the categories are theirs, not editorial positions of this project. Where sources disagree — and on regime type and rights they sometimes do — the map reflects the named source for that layer, and reasonable people can and do dispute those classifications.

The same principle governs borders. Territorial units — Taiwan, Hong Kong, Kosovo, Western Sahara, Palestine, and others — appear as their cited sources report them, sometimes as distinct reporting economies and sometimes consolidated, depending on the dataset. This reflects data provenance. It is not a sovereignty claim.

A note on source quality

Worldbook mixes source tiers, and it's honest about it. Most layers rest on UN agencies, national statistical offices, and peer-reviewed research bodies. A few — particularly some edges in the historical-migration layer, where clean bilateral figures simply don't exist for the 1600s and 1700s — draw on well-established secondary summaries, including encyclopedic sources, on top of primary databases like SlaveVoyages. The app tracks a confidence level per layer. Where the data is softer, the layer says so rather than dressing an estimate up as a hard count.

What this is, and what it isn't

Worldbook is research-grade and approximate. Figures are rounded and, on some layers, blended across the sources named above. It's built for exploration — to make a set of otherwise opaque global systems navigable in one view — not as an authoritative reference you'd cite in a paper. For that, go to the primary sources. They're all linked, on purpose.

Found something wrong?

Good — that's the point. If a corridor looks incorrect, a source is misread, a classification is off, or a flow is missing that the data would support, that's exactly the kind of correction that makes the map better. The whole thing is built to be checkable so that errors are findable. Bring the source and it gets fixed.
